# The Two Truths

**The Two Truths** — the Madhyamaka doctrine that reality admits two valid registers: conventional truth, which describes the stable patterns of lived experience, and ultimate analysis, which finds no intrinsic substance beneath them. Held together, they prevent [[Śūnyatā (Emptiness)|emptiness]] from collapsing into blank denial.

Conventional truth is not a polite fiction. Fire burns, promises bind, institutions distribute power, care changes development, and choices bear consequence. Ultimate analysis does not overturn any of this; it finds only that no isolated substance stands underneath the patterns. Their reality consists in dependent action, not permanent ownership — the claim elaborated as [[Dependent Origination|dependent origination]]. The two registers answer different questions; holding them simultaneously, without letting either cancel the other, is a basic discipline of thought.

The governing analogy is the mirage. Conventional reality is *mirage-like*, not meaningless: a mirage is a real appearance with real causes and possible consequences, and it becomes false only when mistaken for water existing in the way it seems. Likewise, pain is not cancelled because the self in pain is empty of intrinsic existence ([[Anātman (No-Self)|no-self]]); emptiness is precisely what explains how pain can arise, spread, change and end. A world of fixed essences could not alter; a dependently arisen world can — which is why the analysis increases rather than removes the weight of consequence.

The same structure protects value. The good and the bad are like the two banks of a river: each gains form through relation to the other while the river of action runs between them. This does not make moral distinctions arbitrary; it means their meaning depends on a value-bearing ecology rather than an isolated essence, which is how karmic consequence ([[Karma as Contextual Law]]) can run on empty ground. The two truths are also the chief defence against the nihilist misreading of emptiness — the incomplete demolition diagnosed in [[The Nietzsche–Baudrillard–Nāgārjuna Convergence]] — and they recur as a living method in the paired movements of construction and dissolution, [[Svātantrika and Prāsaṅgika]].
