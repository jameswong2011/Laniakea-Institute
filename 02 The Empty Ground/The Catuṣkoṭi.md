# The Catuṣkoṭi

**The Catuṣkoṭi** — Nāgārjuna's four-cornered negation. For any claim, it rules out all four possibilities in turn — that a thing *is*, *is not*, *both is and is not*, or *neither is nor is not* — leaving no corner in which independent, self-contained existence could hide. *Catuṣkoṭi* means "four corners".

The method is a tool, not a belief, so start with how it works. Ordinary logic offers two boxes: a statement is either true or false. The four-cornered logic opens four — true, false, both, neither — and then Nāgārjuna knocks out all four. Take the claim "the self exists as a thing in its own right." Corner one, it exists: but search, and no independent, self-standing self can be found ([[Anātman (No-Self)|no-self]]). Corner two, it does not exist: yet plainly someone is reading this, remembering, continuing. Corner three, it both exists and does not: that only stacks two failed answers together. Corner four, it neither exists nor does not: then we are no longer saying anything. Every corner quietly assumes the thing has *svabhāva* — independent, self-contained being — and every corner collapses. What the collapse reveals is [[Śūnyatā (Emptiness)|emptiness]]: the thing is real, but only as a dependent event ([[Dependent Origination|dependent origination]]).

The negation is deliberately one-directional. A mind still inside the churn of experience cannot draw a positive portrait of reality's source. It can only clear away what reality is *not*, and let what remains show itself. This makes the catuṣkoṭi the working engine of both emptiness and the denial of own-being. It answers the earlier Buddhist analysts without contradicting them: where they named the real, Nāgārjuna names what is *not* real, completing the picture. He spares nothing — even the *tathāgatas*, the fully awakened, fall under dependent origination and are empty of inherent existence.

A subtle payoff appears at the edges of thought. A world of relation all the way down ([[Ontic Structural Realism|structure without substance]]) throws up boundary paradoxes — where does a rainbow end, or a caterpillar become a butterfly? Jay Garfield and Graham Priest argue that the four-cornered logic anticipates a *paraconsistent* handling of these — a logic that can hold a contradiction in one place without the whole system exploding into nonsense. Some contradictions at the limit are true and survivable, not fatal. Classical logic cannot extend that charity.

One caution decides everything. The fourfold negation is not itself a fifth position to stand on. Grasp it as a doctrine and you have missed [[The Emptiness of Emptiness|the emptiness of emptiness]]. It is a ladder to the middle way — and its use as pure demolition, exposing only the other side's contradictions, defines one wing of later Madhyamaka ([[Svātantrika and Prāsaṅgika]]).
