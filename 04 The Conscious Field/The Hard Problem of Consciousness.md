# The Hard Problem of Consciousness

**The Hard Problem of Consciousness** — the question of why physical processing is accompanied by felt experience at all; a puzzle that dissolves once the priority of matter over mind is inverted — at the price of a successor question that stays deliberately open.

In its clean form: a complete inventory of neural firings can describe discrimination, report, attention, memory and behaviour while leaving the felt redness of red or the pain of pain untouched. Explaining the functions is difficult but ordinary science; explaining why performing them feels like anything is different in kind. David Chalmers's paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" gave the problem its contemporary formulation.

The problem is hard only under an assumption there is no reason to make: that matter is primary and mind must be conjured from it. The hard problem was never a puzzle but a category error, put by a materialism that placed effect before cause. Invert the priority — mind as substance, matter as its compressed expression, per [[Consciousness as Primary]] — and [[Qualia|qualia]] need not be produced by neurons at all; they are the native actuality of relation between partitions of consciousness, with the brain as receiver rather than factory ([[Transmission Theory]]). The hard problem is thereby not solved but deprived of its premise, and stands as evidence against the metaphysics that generated it — the faith, rather than the method, distinguished in [[Science and Scientism]].

The exchange is explicit. What dissolution buys: no obligation to derive experience from non-experience, and an ontology in which felt actuality is constitutive rather than anomalous. What it costs: a successor question — how does a capacity without a viewpoint become a particular viewpoint, without creating consciousness from non-consciousness and without declaring every possible partition already a full subject? That question is deliberately left unclosed, filed among [[The Open Tensions]].
